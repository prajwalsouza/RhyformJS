The Statistical Geometry of Invisible Causes

How an astronomer, a brewer, a guinea-pig breeder, a psychologist and a hospital statistician learned to reason from shadows — and why a perfect fit can still be a perfect illusion.

Scroll to read. Each passage moves the scene beside it; when a scene pauses, drag it to look around, and use the sliders where they appear. Or press Watch and let it play like a film.

Picture a man lying in the back of a railway caboose, sometime around 1920, still weak from a serious illness. The car sways and rattles across the American countryside. To pass the time he’s reading a book on quaternions — the strange four-part numbers mathematicians use to describe rotations in three dimensions. Light reading for a train.

At some point, as the story is usually told, he puts the book down, takes out a sheet of paper and starts to draw. Not equations. Guinea pigs. A father guinea pig, a mother guinea pig, their offspring — and between them a web of arrows labelled heredity, environment, and chance.

His name is Sewall Wright, and we’ll come back to him and his guinea pigs. But first notice what he was trying to do: to see something that can’t be seen. You cannot watch heredity happen. You can only see what it leaves behind.

That is what this whole story is about. On the right is a room with a shape floating in it. Until now you haven’t been allowed to see the shape — only the three shadows it throws on the floor and the walls. Here it is.

Almost every tool in modern statistics is a way of reasoning from shadows back to the object that cast them. A scatter plot is a shadow. A bell curve is a shadow. The dashboard in your Monday meeting is a wall covered in shadows. We tend to treat these tools as if they were handed down on stone tablets: eternal, obvious, inevitable. They weren’t. Each was invented by a particular person, stuck on a particular problem, trying to measure something they couldn’t see clearly.

Here is the tension that runs underneath all of it. The shape is gone again, and we’re facing a single wall. That is a perfectly good shadow. It is also consistent with a great many different shapes.

When a model fits the data we can see — fits it perfectly — what have we actually learned about the causes we can’t see?

Does a perfect fit prove the invisible thing is there? The short answer, which took about two hundred years to earn, is no. The long answer is more interesting, because it tells us what would count as evidence.

Our route runs through a French mathematician chasing comets, a brewer in Dublin trying to keep a stout tasting the same, a geneticist doodling guinea pigs on a train, a psychologist hunting for “general intelligence,” a statistician at the Mayo Clinic who noticed that a hospital door can manufacture correlations, and a physicist who convinced the world that atoms were real.

Along the way we’ll build — in three dimensions, where you can walk around them — the ideas underneath path analysis, structural equation modelling, and every regression you have ever run. Let’s start with the sky.

Chapter 1 · Paris, 1805

The mean is a shadow

Adrien-Marie Legendre and the method of least squares

In 1805 Adrien-Marie Legendre was working on one of the hard problems of his day: pinning down the orbit of a comet. Astronomers in different cities, on different nights, each recorded where they saw it — and none of the sightings quite agreed. Air shimmers. Lenses have flaws. Clocks drift, and tired observers write down the wrong minute.

In the scene, the pale dots are sightings and the green ellipse is a proposed orbit. No ellipse can pass through all of them; the points contradict each other. The red lines are the misses: how far each sighting sits from the path. So what is the true orbit, when your evidence disagrees with itself?

Legendre’s answer, tucked into an appendix, was the method of least squares. Take every miss and square it — literally, turn each red line into a square — then add up the areas, and choose the orbit that makes the total as small as possible. Watch the ellipse try a slightly different shape: some squares shrink, others swell. The best orbit is the one where no nudge can make the total smaller.

Gauss later claimed he’d been using the method since 1795, and a famous priority dispute followed. But Legendre published first, and he explained it in a way that matters for our story. He didn’t present it as a trick of arithmetic. He tied it to something physical: in the simplest case, the least-squares answer is the center of gravity of the observations.

Let’s make that concrete, because the humble average hides a surprising amount of geometry. You weigh an object three times and get 1 kg, 2 kg and 6 kg. The usual picture is three dots on a number line.

Put those three readings on a plank as equal weights and ask where the fulcrum has to go for the plank to balance. It wobbles, and settles, at 3. The average is a center of gravity. That is Legendre’s physical intuition, and it’s already a hint that the mean is more than “add them up and divide.” But there is a stranger way to look at it.

Instead of three dots in one dimension, treat the whole data set as a single point in three dimensions. The first reading is how far you walk along one axis, the second how far along another, the third how high you climb.

Walk it: one step forward, two steps to the side, six rungs up a ladder. You arrive at a single glowing point hanging in the air at (1, 2, 6). That point is your whole, messy data set. Three readings make one point in this room; a thousand readings make one point in a room with a thousand dimensions. Nobody can picture a thousand dimensions, but the geometry works exactly the same way — which is why it’s worth learning here, where we can walk around.

Now suppose the object has one true weight — call it a — and your scale were perfect. All three readings would come back the same: (a, a, a). Mark every such point: (1, 1, 1), (2, 2, 2), (3, 3, 3)… They form one straight line, the diagonal of the room.

Call it the line of agreement. Anywhere on it, the three readings agree perfectly. Your data point isn’t on it; it floats off to one side, because your measurements disagree. And so “what single number best represents my data?” has turned into a geometric question: which point on the line is closest to my data point?

Shine a light so your data point casts a shadow straight onto the line, meeting it at a right angle. The shadow lands at (3, 3, 3) — and 3 is the mean of 1, 2 and 6.

That isn’t a coincidence. It is what the mean is. Geometrically, the average is the perpendicular projection of your data onto the line of perfect agreement: the shadow of a messy reality on the line of what a single constant could explain. The red segment, from the data down to its shadow, is the part no constant can explain — the leftover errors (−2, −1, +3). It meets the line at a right angle.

A right angle means a right triangle, and a right triangle means Pythagoras. The squared length of the data, 1² + 2² + 6² = 41, splits exactly into the squared length of its shadow, 3 × 3² = 27, plus the squared length of the miss, 2² + 1² + 3² = 14.

This is why squared error is smallest at the mean. Every other point on the line is farther from your data, because the shortest path from a point to a line is the perpendicular one. Least squares isn’t an arithmetic ritual. It’s the question “what is the nearest point?”, asked in a space where every measurement gets its own direction.

Try it

The first three sliders move your readings, and so the glowing point. The last one slides a candidate constant c along the line. The dark wireframe sphere is centered on your data and always passes through c; its size is the error you would make by claiming the true value is c. It can only shrink until it just touches the line — at the place where the connecting segment meets the line at a right angle. Whatever readings you choose, that place is the mean.

Chapter 2 · The same room, a different ruler

Why square the errors?

Mean, median, and the shape of noise

Here’s the question every student eventually asks, and deserves a better answer to than they usually get. If my errors are −2, −1 and +3, the textbook says I must square them “so the negatives don’t cancel.” But absolute values stop the cancelling too: 2, 1 and 3. So why is squaring the gold standard?

Because squaring isn’t a bookkeeping trick. It is a choice of ruler — a decision about what “close” means — and a different ruler gives a different answer. To see it, take three new readings: 0, 0 and 10. Same room, same line of agreement; the data point now sits high up the third axis.

With squared error, “distance” is ordinary straight-line distance, and the set of all points at one distance from your data is a sphere. So inflate a sphere around the data point and stop the moment it first touches the line of agreement.

It kisses the line at 3.33 — the mean of 0, 0 and 10. That is least squares, seen as a balloon: the mean is the first point of the line the balloon reaches.

Now change the ruler. Measure distance by adding up absolute differences instead. The set of points at one absolute distance from your data isn’t round any more; it’s a diamond — an octahedron, with its corners pointing along the axes.

Inflate the diamond. Its lowest corner reaches the line first, and it reaches it at 0, the median of 0, 0 and 10. Neither 3.33 nor 0 is an arithmetic mistake. They are correct answers to two different questions. Change how you penalise distance and you change what “closest” means.

Try it

The first slider morphs the ruler smoothly: at p = 1 the ball is a diamond, at 2 a sphere, and as p grows it swells into a cube — the ruler that only cares about your single worst miss, whose closest constant is the midrange. The second slider drags the outlier. Watch how the mean chases it and the median simply doesn’t care.

So which ruler is right? That depends on what you believe about the noise. If your errors are independent and follow the classic bell curve — where huge misses are astronomically rare — then the most likely true value, given your data, is exactly the one that minimises squared error. If instead the errors follow a Laplace curve, with a sharper peak and fatter tails, so that wild outliers are a normal part of life, the most likely value minimises absolute error.

Watch the curves turn into penalties. Taking minus the logarithm of the bell gives a parabola (error²); doing the same to the Laplace curve gives a V (|error|). The penalty isn’t arbitrary. It’s the shape of the noise you assumed, turned upside down.

And the bell curve gives squares one more gift. Independent sources of noise add like perpendicular arrows: the spread of their sum is the hypotenuse, so variances — squared spreads — simply add. It is Pythagoras again, this time for uncertainty.

That is the honest answer. We don’t square because negative numbers are annoying. “So they don’t cancel” explains why a raw sum of errors fails — they always add to zero — but not why squares beat absolute values. We square because of the geometry it buys, and because of the Gaussian assumption that makes that geometry the right one.

Chapter 3 · Every statistics class, ever

The missing dimension

Why the sample variance divides by n − 1

While we’re in this room, let’s settle a mystery that haunts every introductory course. To estimate how spread out your data is, you add up the squared residuals — but then, instead of dividing by the number of observations n, the formula makes you divide by n − 1. Everyone memorises it. Few people are ever shown why.

Go back to (1, 2, 6) and its shadow (3, 3, 3). Slide the red miss back to the corner of the room so it starts at zero: the residuals (−2, −1, +3). Because the mean is a center of gravity, the pulls in each direction balance, and the residuals always add up to zero. Tell me the first two, +2 and −3 say, and I can tell you the third: it has to be +1. It isn’t free. It’s locked.

Geometrically, “the three residuals add to zero” is the equation of a plane through the corner of the room, standing exactly perpendicular to the line of agreement. Every residual vector, from every possible data set, lies somewhere on this flat sheet — never above it, never below.

Watch what that does to randomness. Here are 64 independent samples, each of three noisy readings of the same quantity: a fuzzy ball of points hovering around (3, 3, 3), free to wobble in all three directions.

Now center each sample on its own mean. Every point slides along the direction of the diagonal until it hits the plane. The 3D cloud is crushed flat onto a 2D sheet. Nothing was thrown away  — each sample still has three residuals — but they can now only vary in two independent directions.

That is where the “missing” dimension went. You spent one direction of freedom — the one along the line — on estimating the mean. What’s left for measuring spread are the two free directions in the plane. With n readings it’s the same story in a bigger room: one direction pays for the mean, n − 1 remain.

On average, each free direction carries one σ² worth of squared wobble. So the fair way to turn the total squared residual into “spread per direction” is to divide by the number of directions the residual actually lives in. That’s n − 1. You aren’t losing a data point; you’re paying for the mean with a dimension.

Try it

Draw fresh batches and change the true spread. The faint cloud is the raw samples, the red one the same samples after centering — always flat. The numbers below average over thousands of samples, so you can see the three-versus-two appear.

Chapter 4 · Dublin, 1908

The uncertainty of uncertainty

William Sealy Gosset, the Guinness brewery, and “Student”

A century after Legendre, the story moves from the heavens to a brewery. William Sealy Gosset was not a professor. He was a chemist at Guinness in Dublin, with a very practical job: keep the stout tasting the same, batch after batch, year after year, even though the barley it’s made from changes with every field, every summer and every harvest.

His experiments were small — often just three or four samples of barley extract. Each row in the scene is one such sample of three measurements, around a target value. Statisticians of the day worked with thousands of observations, where you can estimate the noise level and then simply treat that estimate as the truth. Gosset couldn’t.

To ask whether a batch really differs from the target, you compute a ratio: how far the sample mean is from the target, divided by how much noise you think there is — the estimated standard error. With three readings, that denominator is itself wildly unstable.

Look at the row where, purely by luck, the three readings happen to agree with each other. Its estimated spread is tiny, so dividing by it makes an ordinary wobble look enormous. A brewer using the usual bell-curve table would declare an anomaly and throw out perfectly good barley. Gosset needed the probability law for the ratio when both the top and the bottom are bouncing around — the uncertainty of his own uncertainty.

Here is the most beautiful way to see what he found — a geometric picture R. A. Fisher later used to prove Gosset’s result. Back in the room, put one sample as a point. Its shadow on the line of agreement measures the mean. Its miss measures the spread. The t-statistic is the first divided by the second, scaled — and a ratio of two sides of a right triangle is just an angle.

For three readings, t = √2 · cot θ, where θ is the angle between the data point and the line of agreement. Small angle, big t. The test doesn’t care how far away the point is. It only cares which direction it points.

So “significant” means “pointing close to the line”: inside a double cone around the diagonal. Now suppose nothing is going on — the true difference is zero and the noise is the same in every direction. Then the data point is equally likely to point anywhere. Each dot on the sphere is the direction of one such null sample.

The false-alarm rate is simply the fraction of the sphere inside the cone — which, by a theorem Archimedes proved about spheres, is 1 − cos θ. For three readings, Student’s threshold of 4.30 makes a cone of 18.2°, and exactly 5% of the sphere falls inside.

Now do what a pre-Gosset brewer would have done: use the normal table’s famous 1.96. The cone swings open to 35.8°, and suddenly 19% of pure noise looks “significant” — nearly four times the promised rate. Narrow it back to Student’s 4.30 and the promise is kept.

That was Gosset’s discovery, in a picture: with tiny samples you must widen your idea of what chance can do. Guinness didn’t allow employees to publish, for fear competitors would learn how scientifically it brewed, so in 1908 he published under a pen name: Student. Every statistics student since has learned “Student’s t-test,” mostly unaware it was invented by a brewer.

Try it

Move the threshold and watch the cone open and close. The percentage is Archimedes’ exact cap area; the count is the dots that actually fall inside.

Here is Student’s whole family at once: one curve for each amount of data, from a single degree of freedom at the back to thirty at the front, with the ordinary bell curve in white. With little data the tails are thick — extreme ratios are common because the denominator is so unreliable. As the sample grows, the estimate of the noise settles down and the curves melt into the bell.

Legendre gave us the geometry of the best estimate. Gosset gave us an honest account of how wrong it can be. But both were measuring one quantity at a time. The world is made of quantities that push on each other.

Chapter 5 · London, 1886

Ellipses and regression

Francis Galton, and a tool with a dark origin

To go from measuring one thing to relating two, we need Francis Galton — and we need to be honest about him. Galton was a gifted and restless scientist, and he was also the founder of eugenics. His statistical work on inheritance was bound up with a programme, which he wrote about openly, to breed a “better” human population. We look at the mathematics here because it outlived its purpose; the purpose itself was a dark chapter of science, and tools forged in it deserve to be handled with that memory.

Galton collected the heights of parents and their grown children and plotted them against each other: parents along one axis, children along the other, one dot per family. A cloud.

Galton noticed that if you count how crowded the cloud is in each region and draw contour lines of equal crowding, like the contour lines on a hiking map, they aren’t squares or random blobs. They are ellipses, nested one inside the other. Raise those contours into a landscape and you get a smooth, stretched mountain: the bivariate normal distribution.

Now look down from above. On each ellipse, find the point where it stands exactly vertical — its farthest reach to the left and to the right. Those tangent points line up, and the line through them is what we now call the regression line: for each parent height, the average height of their children.

It is flatter than the diagonal. Unusually tall parents have tall children, but on average less unusually tall. Galton called it regression toward mediocrity; we say regression toward the mean. The slope of that line is the correlation.

Try it

Change the correlation. A strong link squeezes the mountain into a ridge and tilts the line up to meet the diagonal; no link makes the mountain round and the line lie flat; a negative link swings the ellipses the other way.

Here’s the connection back to Legendre. Regression is the same shadow, one dimension up. Take three families. The children’s heights are one point y in our three-dimensional room; the parents’ heights are another, x. Every prediction of the form “a constant plus a multiple of the parent’s height” lies on the flat plane spanned by the constant direction (1, 1, 1) and x.

The least-squares fit is the point on that plane closest to y: its perpendicular shadow. For the mean we projected onto a line. For simple regression we project onto a plane. For regression with ten predictors, onto an eleven-dimensional flat. The same idea every time.

But notice what the shadow doesn’t tell you. It says tall parents tend to have tall children, and by how much. It says nothing about why — about genes, nutrition, shared homes, or anything that pushes on both. Regression is a tool for prediction. To get from “goes together” to “causes,” someone had to draw a map of the mechanism itself.

Chapter 6 · Washington, 1920

A map made of arrows

Sewall Wright, guinea pigs, and path analysis

Which brings us back to the man in the caboose. Sewall Wright wasn’t trained as a mathematician, and he wasn’t studying people. He was a geneticist at the U.S. Department of Agriculture, breeding guinea pigs and puzzling over their piebald coats — the patchwork of white and coloured fur.

How much of a pup’s pattern came from its parents’ genes? How much from the environment it shared with its littermates in the womb? How much from pure developmental chance as its cells divided? A single correlation between parent and offspring can’t split the resemblance into those buckets. So in his 1920 paper Wright drew what he meant, and his diagram really did include little sketches of guinea pigs, with arrows for heredity, environment and chance.

Then he did something radical. He stripped the pictures down to nodes and gave every arrow a number: a path coefficient. Watch the guinea pigs become abstract nodes X, M and Y. Each arrow now says: “if the thing at my tail moves by one unit, the thing at my head moves by this much.”

Think of each coefficient as the gain on an amplifier. Send a pulse of one unit out of X. It crosses the first arrow and arrives at M multiplied by a. It crosses the second and arrives at Y multiplied by b. Along a chain, effects multiply: the indirect effect of X on Y is a × b.

Add a second route: a direct arrow from X straight to Y, with coefficient c. Now the signal travels both ways at once, and at Y the routes add. The total effect of X on Y is c + a·b. Multiply along paths, add across them — that’s the whole grammar of path analysis, and it turns a biological theory into equations that predict what the observed correlations should be.

Now the profound part. Let a = 1 and b = 1, so the indirect route is a strong +1. And let the direct route be a dampener, c = −1. Total effect: +1 − 1 = 0.

Look at the scatter plot underneath. A passive observer who plots X against Y sees a formless cloud and concludes that X does nothing, and throws it out of the analysis. They would be catastrophically wrong. X is doing a great deal; two mechanisms are pushing against each other exactly.

“Nothing changed” and “nothing happened” are not the same statement.

Two people shoving with all their strength on opposite sides of a door produce a door that doesn’t move. That is a very different door from one nobody is touching.

Try it

Set the three path coefficients yourself. Arrows turn red when negative and thicken with strength; the scatter plot shows only what an observer would see. Find other settings where strong arrows add up to nothing at all.

Not everyone was pleased. Some statisticians and economists of the day saw an animal breeder straying far outside his field, and resisted the method for years. But Wright had done something new. He wasn’t just summarising data. He was proposing a skeleton for how reality worked beneath the surface, with every arrow a hard, testable assumption, and then asking what the data should look like if the skeleton were true.

Guinea pigs, at least, can be seen. What happens when the cause at the center of your diagram is something nobody can see at all?

Chapter 7 · London, 1904

The weight in the net

Charles Spearman, “general intelligence,” and how to test a ghost

In 1904 the psychologist Charles Spearman was studying schoolchildren’s marks in wildly different things: Classics, French, English, mathematics, the ability to tell two musical pitches apart. He noticed something persistent. Every pair of scores was positively correlated. A child above average in Latin tended to be above average in maths, and even at judging pitch.

Each line in the scene is one of those correlations; the thicker, the stronger. Psychologists now call this pattern the positive manifold: all the tests lean the same way.

Spearman proposed an explanation: a single unobserved common cause pushing all the scores up and down together. He called it g, general intelligence, and — crucially — he didn’t leave it as a loose idea. He made it a precise mathematical object: every test score is g times a loading, plus noise of its own.

But hold on. If I believe invisible gremlins in my garden make my tomatoes grow, I can’t just draw an oval, label it “gremlins,” point arrows at the tomatoes and call it science. Drawing a circle doesn’t make the thing inside it real. So how could you ever find evidence for something you can’t observe?

The answer is that the invisible thing has to make risky predictions about the visible world. Start with three tests. Three tests give three correlations, and the one-cause model has three unknown loadings, one per arrow. Three equations, three unknowns: you can always solve them. Here, from Spearman’s own numbers, each loading comes straight out of the correlations.

Which means three tests can never contradict the theory. There’s no leftover information to check it against. A perfect fit here is a tautology, not a discovery.

Almost never, anyway. Each loading squared, λ², is the share of a test’s variance that it holds in common with g, so it must fit inside a container of size one. Now feed in correlations of .80, .80 and .50. The algebra insists the first test shares 1.28 units of variance with g  — which leaves −0.28 for its own unique part. A negative variance, the square of something, below zero.

This is a Heywood case, and software sometimes produces it with perfectly sensible-looking data. The tempting fix is to force the number to zero so the error message goes away. But the negative variance isn’t a glitch; it’s the geometry of the data saying that this causal story cannot produce these numbers. Try the sliders and see which combinations spill.

Now add a fourth test, and everything changes. Four tests give six correlations, but the one-cause model has only four loadings. Two correlations too many: the theory now makes predictions it could get wrong.

Picture a cargo net with four corners, and a heavy, invisible lead weight hanging in its middle. That weight is g. Because every corner is tied to the same weight, the corners can’t pull independently; the tensions are locked in fixed proportions to each other.

Spearman found the lock. If one hidden cause is behind four tests, then the correlation of tests 1 and 2 times that of 3 and 4 must equal the correlation of 1 and 3 times that of 2 and 4 (and of 1 and 4 times 2 and 3). The three bars must be the same height. Their difference, the tetrad difference, must be zero.

Look at that equation. g isn’t in it. A claim about something no one can see has turned into a strict numerical relationship among things you can hold in your hand. Cover up one correlation and the others tell you what it must be. If real data break that equality, the single-cause theory is dead.

Try it

Change the four loadings: the net sags differently, yet the three bars stay equal, because one weight is doing all the pulling. Then add a second hidden cause that only ties tests 1 and 2 together. The bars come apart, and the theory fails its test.

Modern structural equation models are full of ovals like Spearman’s, labelled “socioeconomic status” or “brand loyalty,” with arrows pointing at boxes of survey questions. Is every oval a real hidden cause? No, and mixing that up is one of the most common traps in applied statistics.

What Spearman built is a reflective model. Think of a room’s temperature: a genuine physical state. The thermometers on the walls merely reflect it. If the temperature rises, every reading rises in response. The arrows point out of the hidden thing. Take one thermometer away and temperature means exactly what it meant before.

Now reverse the arrows. A cost-of-living index is computed from rent, food, transport and energy. These aren’t reflections of some invisible “expense substance” radiating from the center of the earth; the cost of living doesn’t cause the rent to rise. Rent rising causes the index to rise. This is a formative, or composite, model, and if you drop rent from it you have changed what the index means.

So drawing an oval and calling it “socioeconomic status” doesn’t endow it with causal power. It might just be a calculator adding up income, education and postcode.

There’s a subtler trap still, laid out by Donald Campbell and Donald Fiske in 1959. Suppose your instruments agree beautifully — the correlations are high, the tetrads are zero, and you declare the hidden truth isolated. But what if all the thermometers are plugged into the same faulty power supply? They agree not because they measure the same deep reality, but because they share the same mistake.

In human data, if three questionnaires all give the same answer, maybe they’ve found a deep trait. Or maybe tired participants ticked “strongly agree” all the way down three similar-looking pages. Campbell and Fiske called it method variance. Reliability is not validity: a wrong measurement, repeated exactly, is perfectly consistent. The pattern in the data is a shadow you still have to explain, not a stamp that certifies whatever name you gave the oval.

Chapter 8 · Rochester, Minnesota, 1946

The hospital door

Joseph Berkson and correlations made out of thin air

So far we can isolate invisible causes, draw arrows, and fit models to the data. Now for the unsettling part: the mathematics will tell you exactly what you ask it — even when you’ve asked the wrong question.

Joseph Berkson, a statistician at the Mayo Clinic, noticed a problem with studies built only on hospital patients. Let’s rebuild his argument with coins. Imagine a building with four equal rooms. Everyone flips two fair, independent coins, X and Z, and goes to the room that matches: heads–heads, heads–tails, tails–heads, tails–tails. A quarter of the people in each. Knowing someone’s X tells you nothing about their Z.

Now make the building a hospital, with one admission rule: you get in if either coin came up heads. The tails–tails room is sent home, and the door closes behind them.

Nothing about the coins has changed. They are exactly as independent as before. The only thing that has changed is who you are allowed to look at.

Walk inside as a researcher and pick a patient at random. Their chart says X came up tails. What can you say about Z? Everything. If they had tails on both they’d have been turned away, so they must have Z heads. Inside the hospital, lacking one trait makes the other a certainty: X and Z have become strongly negatively correlated.

The door manufactured a dependency out of thin air. In causal language, admission is a collider  — a variable with two causes pointing into it — and selecting on a collider opens a fake connection between its causes.

The same happens with degrees of illness. Here are two diseases whose severities are genuinely unrelated: a round cloud. The hospital admits people whose combined severity crosses a threshold. Everyone below the red wall drops out of view, and among the patients who remain, a clear negative slope appears: the sicker you are with one disease, the less sick you tend to be with the other.

Which should make you nervous about the old research habit of “controlling for more variables.” Controlling for — or selecting on — the wrong variable doesn’t remove bias. It can create it.

Try it

Move the admission threshold. Admit everyone and the correlation disappears; admit only the very sick and it grows.

Chapter 9 · Berkeley, 1973

The average that pointed the wrong way

Bickel, Hammel and O’Connell, and Simpson’s paradox

In 1973 the graduate admissions figures at the University of California, Berkeley looked damning. Across the whole university, about 44% of men who applied were admitted, against about 35% of women. A large, statistically significant gap, and on a dashboard, two bars that seem to settle the matter.

The scene stacks the applicants to Berkeley’s six largest departments as blocks — bright for admitted, dark for rejected. Pooled together, the gap is even wider: 45% of men, 30% of women.

Peter Bickel, Eugene Hammel and William O’Connell asked where the decisions were actually made: in the departments. So pull the stacks apart and give each department its own pair of columns.

The story flips. In department A, 62% of men were admitted and 82% of women. Across these six, women did as well as or better than men in four, and the two exceptions are small. Every slice of the pie leans one way; the whole pie leans the other. This is Simpson’s paradox.

How is that even possible? Look at the heights of the columns: where people applied. Most men applied to A and B, which admitted most applicants. Most women applied to C through F, which rejected most applicants of either sex.

The overall rate is a weighted average, and the two groups used completely different weights. It’s like comparing two batting averages when one hitter only ever faced minor-league pitchers and the other faced the best in the world.

Try it

Slide the women’s applications, department by department, toward the pattern men followed, keeping each department’s admission rate for women exactly as it was. Watch the overall gap close, and then reverse.

Now be careful, because this is where people draw the wrong lesson. It does not prove there was no discrimination. It shows you have to ask a precise causal question. Did admissions committees treat equal applicants unequally at the moment of decision? Adjusting for department answers that — it isolates the red arrow.

But why did women apply overwhelmingly to the crowded, underfunded departments in the first place? That’s the path through “department,” and adjusting for department erases it from view. Whether you should control for a variable isn’t a statistical question at all. It depends on which arrows you are trying to measure.

Chapter 10 · Two universes

The chain and the fork

Observational equivalence: when infinite data isn’t enough

Fine, you say. Colliders and paradoxes are traps for the careless. Surely a careful researcher with enough clean data will eventually read the true causal structure straight off the numbers?

Let’s play at being the creator of two universes. In World A, causes run in a chain: X pushes M, and M pushes Y, like falling dominoes. In World B, M is a puppet master: it pulls X’s strings and Y’s strings at the same time, and X does nothing to anything. Two completely different mechanisms.

Now generate data from each: a billion observations of X, M and Y apiece, perfectly clean. Compute their averages and their covariance matrices. Plotted in three dimensions, they make clouds of exactly the same shape.

Not similar. Identical, down to the last decimal place: with the right strengths on the arrows, the chain and the fork produce the very same Gaussian distribution. Give both data sets to the most powerful computer on Earth and it could not tell you which world each came from.

Hand the covariance matrix to structural equation modelling software and propose the chain: it reports a perfect fit. Propose the fork to the same data: perfect fit again. Both worlds agree that once you know M, X and Y have nothing more to say to each other, and that is all passive data can ever reveal here.

The data are completely consistent with two contradictory realities. Watching alone can’t decide which way the arrows point. Is causal modelling doomed, then? Only if all we ever do is watch.

Chapter 11 · Paris, 1908–1913

Many roads to one invisible number

Jean Perrin, and how atoms became real

The first way out comes from physics. At the start of the twentieth century, some serious scientists still regarded atoms as a convenient fiction for balancing equations rather than real objects with size and mass.

Jean Perrin studied Brownian motion: the restless, jittery wandering of tiny grains suspended in water, visible under a microscope. The idea was that the grains are being battered, unevenly, by molecules far too small to see. Each trail in the scene is one grain’s random walk.

Perrin’s genius was not to rely on one experiment. He measured how grains settle with height in a column of liquid, how far they wander sideways, how they rotate — and he gathered other physicists’ results from the blue of the sky, black-body radiation, radioactive decay. For each phenomenon, theory gave an equation containing things you could measure and one thing you couldn’t: the number of molecules in a mole, Avogadro’s number.

Solve each equation for that hidden number, and the answers landed together, all around six to seven hundred thousand billion billion. It is Spearman’s trick again: a hidden quantity forcing strict agreement among things you can see. If atoms weren’t real, that agreement across utterly different mechanisms would be a miracle. By 1913 the holdouts had been convinced, and in 1926 Perrin received the Nobel Prize.

Chapter 12 · Chicago, 1943

Seeing versus doing

Trygve Haavelmo, and the wrench

The second way out is to stop only watching. The Norwegian economist Trygve Haavelmo made the distinction precise in the 1940s, and markets show why it matters. Every day a demand curve and a supply curve cross, and all you ever observe is where they crossed: one price, one quantity. Then tomorrow both curves shift — weather, fashion, costs — and cross somewhere else.

Draw a regression line through a year of those crossings and you get a line, sure. But it’s neither the demand curve nor the supply curve. It’s a line through temporary equilibria. It tells you what prices tend to go with what quantities. It does not tell you what would happen if a regulator forced the price to change.

Back to our two worlds. Instead of watching X, reach in with a wrench: disconnect X from whatever normally causes it, and set it to a value. Judea Pearl later wrote this as do(X).

In the chain, you’ve flicked the first domino: M responds, then Y, and the whole cloud slides. In the fork, X was only a puppet. Grab the puppet and pull, and the puppet master M doesn’t notice; Y stays exactly where it was. Two worlds that were identical in every spreadsheet react completely differently to a wrench.

Try it

Choose a value for X, then switch between watching — looking only at cases where X happened to be near that value — and reaching in to set it. Watching, the two worlds give the same answer. Reaching in, they part.

Conditioning on data and intervening on reality are different mathematical operations. A structural coefficient isn’t a slope through last year’s data; it is a bold claim about what stays stable, and autonomous, when some other part of the system is forced to change. That is far more demanding than a good line of best fit, and far more useful.

Chapter 13 · Today

Why not just use a neural network?

Flexibility is not the same as identification

All this careful machinery — tetrads, colliders, restricted paths — invites an obvious question. Why bother, when a large neural network can swallow a billion data points and learn hidden representations by itself?

Look at the simplest network: an input, one hidden layer, an output. The hidden layer is a set of coordinates the network invents. Now rotate those coordinates, and adjust the next layer’s weights to compensate. The output doesn’t move at all. Every rotation is an equally good network. That is rotational indeterminacy: the hidden representation isn’t pinned down by the inputs and outputs.

Try it

Turn the hidden layer yourself. Whatever you call its axes, the output is untouched.

Put a label like “risk” or “intelligence” on one hidden node and nothing about the network has made that label true. Structural equation models are useful precisely because they take flexibility away: “this indicator measures only this factor” fixes loadings to zero and pins the rotation in place. Restrictions are what produce testable consequences — tetrads that must vanish, variances that must stay positive.

A model that can explain anything explains nothing.

Neural networks are superb tools for prediction. But prediction was never the hard part of this story. The hard part was knowing what the arrows mean.

Epilogue

Some worlds look identical until you touch them

We started with Legendre in 1805, dropping a perpendicular shadow in a room to find the mean. Gosset, in a brewery, learned to account for the uncertainty of his own uncertainty. Galton saw ellipses in a cloud and a line through their tangents. Wright turned guinea-pig sketches into arrows with numbers, and showed that a correlation of zero can hide two mechanisms at war. Spearman made an invisible cause put its neck on the line, with a tetrad that had to vanish.

Then the traps: Berkson’s door, which makes correlations out of nothing; Berkeley’s averages, which point the wrong way; and two worlds, a chain and a fork, that no amount of watching can tell apart. Here are three shadows on a wall. From where we stand, three identical circles.

So the next time someone puts a model with a perfect fit index on the screen and says X causes Y, you’ll know what to ask. All data has a geometry, and a model that fits it may be less a window onto reality than a mirror, reflecting the unchallenged assumptions of whoever drew the arrows. The fit was perfect only because the model had never been forced to make a prediction it could get wrong.

We don’t learn about the invisible by drawing it. We learn by asking what else would have to be true if it were there — and then, whenever we can, by picking up a wrench and letting the world answer.

Some worlds look identical until you touch them.
